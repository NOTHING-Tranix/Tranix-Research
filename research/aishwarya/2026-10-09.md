**Research Topic:** Challenges and Affordable Solutions for Reducing
Postharvest Losses of Fruits and Vegetables in India

## What I Researched

-   I studied why fruits and vegetables get wasted after harvesting and
    what makes it difficult to prevent these losses in India.
-   I reviewed research from ICAR-CIPHET, NABCONS, and other researchers
    to understand the main causes of postharvest losses.
-   I explored the problems farmers face with cold storage,
    transportation, packaging, and handling.
-   I looked into affordable technologies such as Zero-Energy Cool
    Chambers, solar-powered cold storage, reusable crates, and solar
    dryers.
-   I also studied how farmer groups, government schemes, and better
    market connections could help reduce food waste.

## What I Found

### 1. High Cost of Infrastructure

One of the main problems is that building facilities such as
cold-storage rooms, packhouses, and refrigerated vehicles is expensive.
Small farmers often cannot afford these facilities on their own.

Even when storage facilities are available, they may be far from farms,
making them difficult or costly to access.

**Possible solution:** Farmers could share storage facilities through
cooperatives or Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs). This would allow
them to share the cost instead of investing individually.

### 2. High Maintenance and Electricity Costs

Setting up a cold-storage facility is only the beginning. Electricity
bills, maintenance, repairs, and labour also add to the cost.

In some rural areas, unreliable electricity makes the situation more
difficult. Solar energy could help, but installing solar-powered systems
also requires an initial investment.

**Possible solution:** Solar-powered or hybrid cold-storage systems
could reduce dependence on grid electricity. Their cost and suitability
need to be checked before introducing them.

### 3. Damage During Transportation and Handling

Fruits and vegetables can be damaged during harvesting, packing,
loading, unloading, and transportation. Rough handling and unsuitable
packaging can cause bruising and crushing.

Repeated handling while moving produce between farms, markets, and shops
can create additional losses. This means that cold storage alone may not
solve the entire problem.

**Possible solution:** Farmers and workers should be trained to handle
produce carefully. Keeping harvested crops in the shade, using suitable
containers, and avoiding unnecessary handling could help reduce damage.

### 4. Difficulty for Small Farmers in Accessing Storage

Many small farmers do not produce enough to justify building their own
cold-storage facilities. They may also find it difficult to transport
produce to distant storage centres.

Because of this, farmers sometimes need to sell their produce quickly,
even when market prices are low. However, storage is useful only when
its cost is reasonable compared with the benefits.

**Possible solution:** Shared cooling centres near farming areas could
allow farmers to store produce at an affordable price. These centres
should also have good transportation and connections with buyers.

### 5. Zero-Energy Cool Chambers (ZECC)

Zero-Energy Cool Chambers are a low-cost way to keep fruits and
vegetables cooler without conventional refrigeration. They generally use
materials such as bricks, sand, and water to create a cooling effect.

This technology is worth considering because it does not require
conventional electricity and may be useful for farmers who cannot afford
expensive cold storage.

However, it does not work equally well everywhere. Its performance
depends on humidity, water availability, and local weather conditions.

**Possible solution:** ZECCs could be introduced in suitable farming
areas for short-term storage, after checking whether they work well for
the crops grown there.

### 6. Solar-Powered Cold Storage

Solar-powered cold storage uses energy from sunlight to operate cooling
equipment. It could be useful in areas where electricity supply is
unreliable.

This technology can help preserve produce, but installation,
maintenance, and battery replacement costs can be challenging.

**Possible solution:** Shared solar-powered storage facilities could be
established near farming communities. Several farmers could use the same
facility and share the expenses.

### 7. Better Packaging with Reusable Crates

Packaging plays a major role in preventing damage. When fruits and
vegetables are packed in unsuitable containers or sacks, they can get
crushed during transportation.

Reusable ventilated crates and protective liners can provide better
protection and reduce bruising in suitable situations.

However, these materials also have costs, and reusable crates need to be
cleaned, collected, and returned after use.

**Possible solution:** Farmer groups and transport operators could
introduce a shared crate system. They should first test whether the
reduction in damage justifies the additional cost.

### 8. Solar Drying and Food Processing

Sometimes farmers cannot sell all their fruits and vegetables before
they begin to spoil. In such cases, drying or processing suitable
produce could help reduce waste.

Solar dryers use sunlight to remove moisture and help preserve products
for longer. This could create opportunities to sell dried fruits and
vegetables instead of depending entirely on fresh markets.

However, farmers would still need proper hygiene, packaging, processing
facilities, and customers who are willing to buy these products.

**Possible solution:** Small-scale solar drying and processing units
could be introduced in farming communities where enough surplus produce
and market demand are available.

### 9. Storage Facilities May Not Be Used Throughout the Year

Storage facilities may receive large quantities of produce during
harvest seasons but remain underused during other periods.

When fewer farmers use a facility, its operating costs are spread across
a smaller quantity of produce. This can make storage more expensive for
each farmer.

**Possible solution:** Storage centres could serve several farmer groups
and suitable crops, where practical. Planning around harvest seasons and
finding reliable buyers could help these facilities operate more
efficiently.

### 10. Lack of Training and Coordination

Reducing postharvest losses requires cooperation between farmers,
transporters, storage operators, wholesalers, retailers, and government
agencies.

Without proper training, workers may continue using methods that damage
produce. Poor coordination can also lead to delays, unnecessary
handling, and difficulties in finding buyers.

**Possible solution:** Practical training and better communication
between everyone involved in the supply chain could improve how fruits
and vegetables are handled and transported.

### 11. Challenges in Accessing Government Schemes

Government schemes can support agricultural infrastructure, storage
facilities, food processing, and farmer organisations. However, some
farmers may find it difficult to understand the schemes, complete
applications, arrange finance, or identify suitable facilities.

Financial support alone may not be enough if a facility is too far away
or too expensive to operate.

**Possible solution:** Farmers and FPOs could receive guidance on
finding suitable schemes, applying for assistance, planning
infrastructure projects, and connecting with buyers.

Programmes to investigate include the Agriculture Infrastructure Fund
(AIF), Mission for Integrated Development of Horticulture (MIDH),
Agricultural Marketing Infrastructure (AMI), and e-NAM. Current
eligibility rules and benefits should be checked through official
sources.

### 12. Need for Better Data on Postharvest Losses

Different research studies report different loss estimates because they
may focus on different crops, locations, and stages of the supply chain.

For example, losses during harvesting are not the same as losses
measured across the entire journey from farm to consumer. Physical
damage and loss of quality may also be measured differently.

**Possible solution:** Losses should be measured separately during
harvesting, storage, transportation, wholesale marketing, and retail.
This would help identify where the biggest problems occur and which
solutions are working.

## My Thoughts

-   I think the first step should be to reduce damage during harvesting,
    packaging, and transportation before investing heavily in expensive
    technologies.
-   A possible opportunity could be setting up shared cooling centres
    near farming areas so small farmers can access storage without
    paying the full cost themselves.
-   Solar-powered storage could be useful in areas with electricity
    problems, but it is important to check whether farmers can afford
    the operating costs.
-   There is no single solution for every situation. ZECCs may work in
    some climates, while refrigerated storage may be more suitable for
    other crops and longer storage periods.
-   Solar drying could be useful for surplus produce when farmers cannot
    sell everything in fresh markets. However, there must be buyers for
    the processed products.
-   Better packaging and careful handling may seem like small
    improvements, but they could make a difference throughout the supply
    chain.
-   Before choosing a technology, we should compare its total cost with
    the value of the produce it can save. Buying a system is not enough
    if farmers cannot afford to maintain and use it.
-   Overall, combining simple handling improvements with suitable
    storage, processing, and transportation methods seems more practical
    than depending on just one solution.

## Overall Conclusion

From this research, I understood that reducing postharvest losses is not
just about building more cold-storage facilities. Farmers also need
better packaging, careful handling, affordable transportation, suitable
storage technologies, and access to markets.

Expensive technologies may help in some situations, but low-cost
improvements can also make a difference. The best approach is to
understand the problems farmers face in each region and choose solutions
that are practical, affordable, and easy to maintain.
