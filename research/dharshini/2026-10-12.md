Research Topic: Real-Time Monitoring and Machine Learning-Based Prediction of Tomato Spoilage Risk During Transportation

 What I Researched
- Reviewed existing research on tomato spoilage and post-harvest losses during storage, handling, and transportation, particularly in the Indian supply chain.
- Studied how IoT sensors monitor temperature and humidity, how GPS records location and transport delays, and how computer vision identifies ripeness, visible damage, and freshness.
- Examined machine-learning approaches for current quality classification, remaining shelf-life estimation, and future spoilage-risk prediction.
- Checked public tomato image datasets and whether they include transport information such as GPS, temperature, humidity, vibration, and destination quality outcomes.

 What I Found
- Finding 1: Tomato quality can be affected by initial ripeness, handling, packaging, vibration, transit time, temperature, and humidity. Loss estimates also depend on how “loss” or “spoilage” is defined.
- Finding 2: IoT sensors monitor environmental conditions, GPS records routes and stops, and computer vision classifies visible ripeness or damage. These technologies provide different information and need to be linked to the same batch and timeline.
- Finding 3: Public tomato datasets identified in the review are mainly image-based. They can support ripeness or visible-quality classification, but generally lack transport history and verified spoilage outcomes for a reliable transport-specific risk model.
- Finding 4: The reviewed literature did not establish a single validated system combining GPS, in-transit temperature and humidity, vibration, checkpoint images, and destination spoilage outcomes for Indian road transportation.
- Finding 5: A proposed framework is to register and inspect each batch, monitor transport conditions, capture checkpoint images, align records by time and batch, and compare predictions with quality assessments on arrival. This is proposed research, not an implemented or experimentally validated system.

 My Thoughts
- I think combining transport-condition data with images of the same tomato batch could provide a more complete picture than using images or sensor readings alone.
- A possible opportunity could be to collect an affordable, batch-level dataset from Indian tomato transport routes, including temperature, humidity, GPS stops, checkpoint images, and clearly defined quality outcomes.
- One thing we should consider is that GPS does not directly measure spoilage, and image-based freshness classification does not automatically predict how soon tomatoes will spoil. A model needs reliable time-stamped data and independently measured outcomes.
- It may be practical to begin with monitoring and alerts for unusual heat or long delays, then develop shelf-life and spoilage-risk prediction after sufficient field data is collected.

 Questions / Doubts
- Is there a public dataset combining Indian tomato transport GPS records, temperature, humidity, vibration, images, and destination quality labels?
- How can spoilage be defined consistently—for example, visible decay, weight loss, commercial rejection, or time until a quality threshold is reached?
- Can low-cost sensors and checkpoint photographs provide reliable data under real Indian road, weather, lighting, and handling conditions?
- How much field data is needed to validate a model across different tomato varieties, seasons, routes, and vehicles?
